Yes — foreigners can buy property in Berlin. Germany does not generally restrict residential property ownership by nationality. EU and non-EU buyers purchase under the same civil-law framework as German buyers.

What differs in practice is financing, equity requirements, tax setup, power of attorney at the notary, and planning for purchase costs. This guide walks through how to buy an apartment in Berlin as a foreign buyer — from goals to land-register transfer.

Can foreigners buy property in Berlin without special permission?

For residential property: yes, in general. There is no special “foreign buyer licence” for a normal apartment purchase.

Friction usually comes from process, not from a nationality ban:

  • Banks often ask non-residents for higher equity
  • Tax and filing duties must be structured correctly
  • Company acquisitions trigger transparency-register rules
  • Notary language and timelines need clear representation

With local legal and tax support early, foreign buyers complete purchases every week in Berlin.

Why Berlin attracts international buyers

Berlin offers European capital-city living, deep rental demand, and — after the 2022–2024 market reset — often clearer entry pricing than at the previous peak. Owner-occupiers look for lifestyle and transport; investors look for durable tenant demand and a long hold.

How to buy an apartment in Berlin: step by step

1. Define goals and budget

Owner-occupier or investment? Tenanted or vacant? How much equity covers the price and closing costs? Many international buyers budget only for the purchase price and underestimate transfer tax, notary and agent fees.

2. Prepare financing

Foreign buyers can often get a German mortgage, but terms differ from local residents. Expect higher equity (often roughly 20–40%, lender-dependent), more documentation, and longer underwriting. Get a realistic financing indication before you bid hard.

3. Choose district and building

Berlin is a set of micro-markets. [[landing:berlin_charlottenburg|Charlottenburg|inline]] and [[landing:berlin_prenzlauer_berg|Prenzlauer Berg|inline]] are typically more expensive and stably demanded; [[landing:berlin_friedrichshain_kreuzberg|Friedrichshain-Kreuzberg|inline]] and [[landing:berlin_neukoelln|Neukölln|inline]] can mean different yield and risk profiles. Buy location and building quality — including HOA (WEG) papers and the energy certificate.

4. Due diligence and notary

Review land register, encumbrances, lease (if rented), house dues and reserves. The purchase contract is notarised — in person or via notarised power of attorney, which is common for overseas buyers.

5. Payment, transfer tax, land register

After signing, you pay under the contract terms, settle transfer tax, and wait for ownership registration. Weeks can pass between notarisation and registration. Use a practical checklist: what to check before buying an apartment in Berlin.

Mortgages for foreign buyers

Not every bank treats non-residents the same. Plan for:

  • higher equity requirements
  • slower document review
  • possible loan-to-value limits

Some buyers purchase with cash and refinance later. That can simplify closing, but it changes liquidity planning. Confirm lending before you fall in love with a unit.

What costs should buyers expect?

In Berlin, closing costs often total about 10–12% of the purchase price when an agent is involved. The largest single item is 6% real estate transfer tax (Grunderwerbsteuer), plus notary/land register and any buyer agent fee. Full breakdown: hidden costs of buying property in Berlin / Kaufnebenkosten.

Banks usually do not finance these costs — keep them as equity.

Tenant rules matter for investors

If you buy rented, German tenancy law is protective of tenants. Existing rents, notice rules and modernisation follow a different playbook than in many other countries. Read Berlin rental laws explained for property buyers before you buy a tenanted investment.

Common mistakes foreign buyers should avoid

  • Underestimating closing costs
  • Choosing a district on hype alone
  • Ignoring renovation and capex, especially in Altbau
  • Buying without local legal/tax advice
  • Organising power of attorney and payment timelines too late

Berlin property buyer checklist

  • Goal clear: own use / investment / rented / vacant
  • Equity includes closing costs
  • Financing framework confirmed
  • District comps reviewed
  • Land register, lease, WEG, energy certificate checked
  • Notary and power of attorney organised
  • Tax setup agreed with an advisor

FAQ

Can non-EU citizens buy property in Berlin?

Yes. There is no general nationality ban on normal residential purchases.

Do I need to live in Germany to buy?

No. Many international buyers close from abroad, often with a notarised power of attorney.

Do I need a German tax ID?

You will need the correct German tax registration for duties such as rental income. Arrange this with a tax advisor before or right after purchase.

How long does buying take?

From serious offer to land-register entry, plan for several weeks — longer if financing and overseas documents are involved.
